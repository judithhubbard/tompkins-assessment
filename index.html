Tompkins Assessment Audit

Tompkins County assessment audit

Assessments 2021–2026 · about 36,000 properties · 3,851 sales 2021–2025 · September 2026

Please note: this is an informal analysis of public data by a private citizen, not a professional appraisal or ratio study. It is offered as a basis for discussion and for questions to put to the Department of Assessment. It should not be relied on directly for decisions; figures should be verified by the county or an independent firm before any action is taken on them.

How to read the numbers

0.80 means the assessment was 80% of the sale price.

"Before" is the assessment when the property sold. "After" is the first assessment set once the county knew the price.

If assessments were fair, the figure would be about the same for every type of property and every part of the county.

Your tax bill is your assessment times the tax rate. The rate is what the county, towns and schools need, divided by everyone's assessments. If some properties are assessed too low, everyone else pays more. This audit checks whether that is happening in Tompkins County, using actual sale prices.

  1. House assessments rose 40% from 2021 to 2024. Apartments rose 16%, commercial property 7%, vacant land 10%, farmland 1%.
  2. A house that sold was reset to its sale price; its unsold neighbors were not. Buyers' assessments rose about 26%; similar houses nearby rose 7–10%.
  3. Apartments and commercial property are assessed at about 0.79 and 0.63 of what they sell for; houses at 0.88.
  4. Accuracy varies by town: 0.74 in outer Groton, 0.94 in Cayuga Heights.
  5. Cheap houses are assessed at nearly full price: under $150,000, 0.91–0.98; everything above, about 0.80.
  6. Some large properties look wrong, including a shopping center that sold for three times its assessment.
  7. Collegetown Terrace is assessed at $85.8 million. Its owners borrowed $185 million against it.
  8. Result: homeowners pay roughly $9 million a year that should be paid by owners of apartments, commercial property and vacant land — the sales support anywhere from $2 million to $17 million.

1. Houses were raised every year; nothing else was

Houses were revalued every year — by a flat percentage in 2022 and 2023, house by house in 2024. Everything else was mostly left alone: in most years more than 90% of commercial, vacant and farm parcels kept exactly the same number, and apartments were touched only in 2022 and 2024. Before 2021 the county revalued towns in turn, every two to three years. In 2025 and 2026 it froze all assessments while it changes computer systems.

Why it matters: when only houses are revalued, houses pay a bigger share of the tax every year, whether or not their value grew faster.

2. Houses that sold were reset to the sale price

When a house sold, its next assessment was set to the sale price — exactly, in 41% of 2023 sales. Unsold neighbors got the general increase and stayed about 20% below market. The county's own annual reports show the same pattern every year since 2017, when they began reporting both figures: house assessments go in below sale prices and come out matching them.

Why it is not allowed. State law requires every property on a roll to be assessed at the same percentage of value (RPTL §305). Raising only the properties that sold — "welcome stranger" assessing — has been struck down by the U.S. Supreme Court as a denial of equal protection (Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal v. Webster County, 1989) and by New York courts as selective reassessment (Krugman v. Board of Assessors, 1988), and the state's own guidance to assessors says the same (ORPTS Opinion of Counsel 10-60). A buyer assessed at 100% while comparable neighbors sit at 80% is entitled to have the assessment cut to 80% (RPTL §§522, 524).

Why it matters: a buyer pays about 15–17% more tax than a neighbor with an identical house. And because the state checks the county against these same sales, the county looks more accurate than it is.

3. Apartments and commercial property are under-assessed

Houses are brought up to the sale price after they sell. Apartment buildings and commercial property are not: the county records the sale and leaves the assessment roughly where it was. Its annual reports say this is deliberate.

What the county says

The answer

It "does not look at sales ratios" for commercial property: many prices reflect leases, tax strategies or hopes of future gains, not the building's current income.

Partly fair. A store with a long lease is priced on the lease; a hotel or furnished student building's price includes furniture and business value, which are not taxed. Setting those aside is normal. But the county rejects commercial prices as unreasonable while setting house assessments to the sale price to the dollar. The law requires one standard for everyone.

Buyers of student housing pay more than the rents justify, so it values those buildings on their income instead.

The law does not allow that. Value is what buyers actually pay. The court-approved way to value a rental building uses the rate of return buyers actually accept. If buyers of Collegetown apartments accept a 4% return, that is the market, and applying it to the rents gives roughly the sale prices.

Result: after they sell, apartment buildings are assessed at 0.80 of price, commercial property at 0.85, and the large Collegetown buildings at 0.5–0.6. Houses are assessed at 0.99 — the sale price, to the dollar, in four cases out of ten.

Assessment as a share of sale price, by type of property, all 2021–25 sales

Assessors' standards (IAAO) say every type should sit between 0.90 and 1.10 of sale price. "Spread" is how much assessments vary within a group before the sale; lower is better. The limit is wider for commercial property and land because they are harder to value — it allows more scatter, not a lower level. Bold marks a failure. Farmland rests on 24 sales.

Why it matters: apartments and commercial property are about $2.5 billion of the tax base. Valuing every type at what its sales since July 2024 say it is worth, and holding every county, town and school levy where it is, about $9 million a year of tax moves off houses and onto apartments, commercial property and vacant land. The sales support anywhere from $2 million to $17 million, because only 25 commercial and 42 apartment buildings sold in those two years; and the largest complexes, which almost never sell, are not measured at all.

4. Accuracy differs by town and neighborhood

The current roll (frozen since 2024) tested against sales from July 2024 to May 2026, using the assessment that was already on the books when each house sold. The Village of Freeville is left out (too few sales). Cayuga Heights reached 0.94 only in the 2024 revaluation; it was at 0.73–0.75 in 2022–23. Neighborhoods range even more widely, from 0.74 to 0.96.

Why it matters: county and school taxes are shared across town lines. A homeowner in Cayuga Heights (0.94) pays about 28% more county and school tax per dollar of real value than one in outer Groton (0.74). The state's equalization rate and the county's own reports put the county at 100% of market value; the sales say 0.88.

5. Cheap houses are assessed at nearly full price; everyone else at about 80%

Houses that sold for under $100,000 were assessed at 0.98 of the price; $100,000–150,000 at 0.91; everything from $150,000 to $1 million at about 0.80; and houses over $1 million lower still, at 0.77 — with most of the lakefront houses among them at 0.40–0.68. Some cheap sales are not market sales — estates, foreclosures, transfers within families at below-market prices — and those push the cheapest group's ratio up, because a low price makes the ratio high. A check for same-surname sales found only 21 and removing them changed nothing, but estate and foreclosure sales cannot be identified in this data. The pattern holds in four of five years, and the $100,000–150,000 group, where such sales are rarer, is also well above the rest.

Why it matters: owners of the least expensive homes pay the highest effective tax rate in the county, and owners of the most expensive — especially lakefront — homes pay the lowest.

6. Properties that deserve a second look

Sales of $1 million or more where the assessment stayed below 70% of the price
Assessments over $1 million cut by more than 25% in a single year

Some cuts will have good reasons — a court settlement, a demolition, a tax agreement with the county's development agency (roll section 8 means fully tax-exempt) — and each should have a written explanation on file. Also, about 100 taxable homes kept exactly the same assessment from 2021 to 2024 while their towns rose 25–40%, including a $1.6 million lakefront home.

Fraternity and sorority houses. 50 taxable houses in the City and Cayuga Heights, $45 million in all; 90% have had the same assessment since 2021. They rarely sell (three sales in ten years; 210 Thurston Ave sold for $2.5 million in 2016 and is assessed at $1.8 million). They are assessed at a median $112 per square foot; the apartment buildings in the same neighborhoods are at $203, and City apartment buildings sell at $204.

Why it matters: the South Meadow Street shopping center alone — sold for $24.25 million, assessed at $10.5 million — shifts roughly $400,000 a year onto other city and school taxpayers.

7. Collegetown Terrace

Five tax parcels on East State Street and Valentine Place, owned by three related companies (VVA Phase I & II, III and IV — the Novarr-Mackesey group). All are fully taxable apartment buildings, built in phases from 2013 to 2017: about 770 apartments by the county's count, more than 1,250 beds by the developer's.

Collegetown Terrace: the five parcels and their assessments ($ millions)
Two cautions

Furniture, the shuttle and services are not taxable real estate, so an assessment can sit somewhat below a sale price or loan — but not at half.

Cost is not value. The $190 million construction cost is background only; the mortgage and the rent carry the weight.

Three checks all say the assessment is low:

  • The mortgage. Lenders lend 65–80% of appraised value, so a $185 million loan implies an appraisal of $230–285 million in 2019. Fannie Mae loans require an independent appraisal from the building's income, and net income of at least 1.25 times the loan payments.
  • What similar buildings sell for. Large Collegetown apartment buildings sold in 2021–25 for $310,000–$720,000 per apartment. Collegetown Terrace is assessed at about $111,000 — less than a garden-apartment complex in Lansing.
  • The rent it earns. Advertised rents run from $599 per bedroom per month (a small studio suite with shared bathroom and kitchen) to $2,259 (a one-bedroom cottage). Valued from that income as a buyer would, the property is worth roughly $115–220 million. For $85.8 million to be right, it would have to net only about $5 million a year.

Allowing for the cautions, the assessment is probably $50–130 million too low.

Why it matters: at the city's combined tax rate ($32.28 per $1,000 including school tax) that is $1.6–4.2 million a year paid by other city and school taxpayers. The Legislature cannot set an assessment, but it can have the five parcels valued from their income. (Owners need not share income figures unless they challenge their assessment.)

8. What the law requires

  • One standard for all. Real Property Tax Law §305 requires every property to be assessed at the same percentage of market value. There is no lower standard for commercial property. Apartments at 0.79 and commercial property at 0.63 while houses are at 0.88 does not meet it.
  • Rental buildings are valued from their rent. The state's highest court: income property is valued by what an investor would pay for its income (Merrick Holding, 1978), and a recent normal sale is the best evidence of value (A&P v. Kiernan, 1977). The properties in section 6 are assessed at 0.23–0.70 of prices they actually sold for; whatever method produced those figures did not treat the sale as the best evidence.
  • Only the owner can complain. An assessment is presumed correct (FMC v. Unmack, 1998), and only a person aggrieved by it — in practice the owner — may challenge it (RPTL §§524, 704). No one can complain that a neighbor is assessed too low. So large commercial properties stay under-assessed for years, and only the Legislature is in a position to act.
  • Resetting only the houses that sold is illegal. Selective reassessment of sold properties violates equal protection (Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal v. Webster County, U.S. Supreme Court 1989) and New York law (Krugman v. Board of Assessors, 1988). A homeowner raised to the sale price while comparable neighbors stay at 80% has grounds for an "unequal assessment" complaint (§§522, 524). Section 2 describes roughly 1,200 such cases.

9. What the Legislature should do

The freeze. Assessments have been frozen since 2024 while the Department changes software, and its plan returns to full value only in 2029: commercial property valued in 2028, houses and apartments in 2029. The freeze does not pause the problem — the 2024 pattern (houses 0.88, apartments 0.79, commercial 0.63 of what they sell for) stays in place, about $9 million a year, until it is corrected. It did stop the resetting of sold houses, which will resume in 2029 unless a rule prevents it.

Some of this only the Director of Assessment can do. The rest the Legislature can do itself, whether or not the Assessment Department engages. The sequence: 2026, commission the check and the valuation; 2027, pass the law; 2028, apartments and commercial property brought to the same level as houses; 2029, the full revaluation under the new rules.

To ask of the Director of Assessment

  • Bring apartments and commercial property to the same percentage of market value as houses on the 2028 roll. The law requires a uniform percentage, not 100%; while houses stay at 0.88, apartments and commercial property should be at 0.88 too. The Department already plans to value commercial property in 2028 — add apartments, instead of leaving them to 2029.
  • Do not resume raising only the house that sold when houses are revalued in 2029. If a sale shows a neighborhood is under-assessed, raise the neighborhood; otherwise give the sold house the same increase as its neighbors.
  • Look at the places furthest below market — outer Groton, Enfield, the Village of Groton and Trumansburg — and stop giving Cayuga Heights and the City the same increase as the outer towns.
  • Check how the cheapest houses are valued.
  • Explain or fix each property in section 6. For Collegetown Terrace, state the rent, expenses and capitalization rate behind $85.8 million.
  • Publish a sales-ratio study of the roll each fall, in this form: for every sale since the valuation date, the assessment that was on the roll before the sale, divided by the price; the median ratio, spread and number of sales for every type of property, every municipality and every assessment neighborhood; and the sales list itself, with the arm's-length code for each sale. The Department has all of this in its database. Its annual reports now give a ratio for houses countywide, two ways. The before-revaluation figure is a fair test, and by the Department's own numbers it was 0.73 for the 2023 roll and 0.81 for 2024. The after-revaluation figure (1.04, 1.02, and 0.97 in the 2025 report) compares the new roll with the same sales it was built from — a quarter of those houses are assessed at exactly their sale price — so it measures fit, not accuracy. The reports give no figures by municipality or neighborhood, and none for apartments or commercial property.

What the Legislature can do itself

  • Require the sales-ratio study above as a condition of the Assessment Department's budget, and have an outside firm verify it each year (a few thousand dollars). If the Department will not produce it, an outside firm can, from the public files, for about $15,000–40,000. Send it to the state Office of Real Property Tax Services with a request that it review its own figure for the county.

  • Hold the Department to results, not promises, by local law. The Department says it revalues every year; the sales show what that produced. The Legislature cannot set a property's value, but it chooses the reassessment schedule, pays for it, and can define what counts as done. Amend the county's Administrative Code, in time for the 2028 roll, so that any type of property, town or neighborhood whose ratio differs from the county's overall ratio by more than 5 points (on at least 20 sales over two years) must be revalued on the next roll, with the funding to follow. That is the test that matters: the law requires one percentage for everything, so if the county is at 0.88, every type and every town must be between 0.83 and 0.93. Whether the whole county is within 0.90–1.10 of its claimed level is the second test.

    Types with too few sales — fraternities, hotels, industrial property, the large student complexes — are tested by appraisal instead: an outside appraiser values a sample every three years, and the same 5-point rule applies. Run the check each fall, before the budget; publish it with the sales list; send it to the state; and let owners cite it in grievances.

  • Fund an independent valuation of every apartment and commercial property now. This report is the first year's check, and those two types fail it. Hire an appraisal firm to value all of them — about 2,250 parcels, $2.5 billion of assessed value — from their income at market rents, with full appraisals of the largest, starting with Collegetown Terrace and the section 6 properties. Roughly $0.5–1 million, one time, against a shift of roughly $9 million a year — of which about $1.7 million is county tax, $2.1 million town and city tax, and $5.4 million school tax. Write the contract so the Department must adopt the values or explain in writing, parcel by parcel, why not, and put the appraisals on the public record.
  • Ask the State Comptroller to audit the assessment function. Requested by resolution, at no cost; the audit produces public findings and the county must file a written corrective action plan within 90 days (General Municipal Law §35). The Comptroller has audited dozens of assessors' offices, though nearly all on exemption administration rather than valuation, so name the scope carefully: whether commercial and apartment assessments have a written, documented basis; how sold properties are treated; and whether the Department's published statistics are supported by its records. Questions of valuation itself may be referred to the state Office of Real Property Tax Services. Six to twelve months, so a 2027 result.
